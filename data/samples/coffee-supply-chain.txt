Coffee begins as the seed of a fruit. The coffee plant, a shrub of the genus Coffea, produces cherries that ripen from green to red over several months. Two species dominate world production: Coffea arabica, which is grown at higher altitudes and prized for its sweetness and acidity, and Coffea canephora, known as robusta, which tolerates heat and disease, contains roughly twice as much caffeine and supplies most instant coffee and many espresso blends. Arabica originated in the highlands of Ethiopia, which is still regarded as the birthplace of coffee, while robusta is native to central and western Africa.

Brazil is the largest coffee producer, followed by Vietnam, which grows mainly robusta, and Colombia, whose arabica is marketed by the Colombian Coffee Growers Federation. Most of the world's coffee is grown by smallholder farmers in the tropical belt between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, often on farms of less than five hectares. Many smallholders sell through cooperatives, which pool harvests, provide credit and negotiate with exporters.

Harvesting is either selective, in which pickers take only ripe cherries by hand, or strip harvesting, in which all cherries are removed at once, sometimes by machine on the flat plantations of Brazil. Processing then removes the fruit from the seed. In the washed process, a depulper strips the skin, the beans ferment in tanks for one to two days so that enzymes break down the sticky mucilage, and the beans are washed and dried on patios or raised beds. In the natural process, whole cherries are dried in the sun for several weeks, which imparts fruity flavours. The honey process, common in Costa Rica, dries the beans with some mucilage still attached. Dried beans, called parchment coffee, are stored until milling removes the parchment layer and yields green coffee.

Green coffee is graded by size, density and defect count, and cuppers evaluate samples for aroma, acidity, body and flavour. Exporters ship green coffee in jute bags or bulk containers to importing countries; the port of Santos in Brazil and the port of Antwerp in Belgium handle large volumes. The commodity price of arabica is set on the Intercontinental Exchange in New York, while robusta trades on the London exchange. Certification schemes such as Fairtrade and Rainforest Alliance set minimum prices or environmental standards, and specialty roasters increasingly buy through direct trade relationships with individual farms.

Roasting transforms green coffee. In a drum roaster the beans reach temperatures between 180 and 250 degrees Celsius. Around 150 degrees the Maillard reaction between sugars and amino acids produces hundreds of aromatic compounds and the brown colour; at the first crack, near 196 degrees, steam and carbon dioxide burst the bean structure. Light roasts preserve origin character and acidity, while dark roasts develop bitterness and oils on the surface. Roasted coffee stales quickly because carbon dioxide escapes and oils oxidise, so roasters pack beans in bags with one-way valves.

Brewing extracts soluble compounds from ground coffee with hot water. Espresso forces water at about nine bars of pressure through finely ground coffee in under thirty seconds, producing a concentrated shot topped with crema. Filter methods such as pour-over and drip use coarser grounds and gravity. The French press steeps coffee for about four minutes before a metal mesh separates the grounds. Cold brew steeps coarse grounds in cold water for twelve to twenty-four hours. Baristas adjust grind size, dose, water temperature and contact time to control extraction; under-extracted coffee tastes sour and over-extracted coffee tastes bitter.

Caffeine, the alkaloid that makes coffee stimulating, is removed in decaffeinated coffee by the Swiss Water process, which uses carbon filters, or by supercritical carbon dioxide extraction. The spent grounds and the pulp from processing are increasingly composted or used as fuel, and shade-grown coffee, planted under a tree canopy, supports birds and reduces the need for fertiliser.
