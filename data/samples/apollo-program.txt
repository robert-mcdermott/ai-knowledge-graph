The Apollo program was the United States human spaceflight program that landed the first people on the Moon. NASA conceived Apollo in 1960 as a follow-on to Project Mercury, but its goal was fixed on 25 May 1961, when President John F. Kennedy told Congress that the nation should commit itself to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth before the end of the decade. The announcement came weeks after Yuri Gagarin of the Soviet Union became the first human in space and Alan Shepard made the first American suborbital flight.

NASA chose lunar orbit rendezvous as the mission mode in 1962, largely through the advocacy of engineer John Houbolt of the Langley Research Center. Under this plan a Saturn V rocket would launch a command and service module together with a separate lunar module; the lunar module would descend to the surface while the command module remained in lunar orbit. The Saturn V was developed at the Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama, under Wernher von Braun, and its F-1 engines were built by Rocketdyne. North American Aviation built the command and service module, Grumman built the lunar module on Long Island, and the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory designed the Apollo Guidance Computer, whose flight software was led by Margaret Hamilton. Launches took place from Launch Complex 39 at the Kennedy Space Center in Florida, and flights were directed from Mission Control at the Manned Spacecraft Center in Houston, later renamed the Johnson Space Center. Project Gemini, flown between 1965 and 1966, tested rendezvous, docking and spacewalks that Apollo would depend on.

The program suffered its worst setback on 27 January 1967, when a fire in the command module during a launch rehearsal killed astronauts Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee. The mission was later designated Apollo 1. The investigation led to a redesigned hatch, fireproof materials and a cabin atmosphere that was no longer pure oxygen at launch. Apollo 7, commanded by Wally Schirra in October 1968, was the first crewed flight and tested the command module in Earth orbit. In December 1968 Apollo 8 carried Frank Borman, Jim Lovell and William Anders around the Moon; they became the first humans to leave Earth orbit and see the far side of the Moon, and Anders took the photograph known as Earthrise. Apollo 9 tested the lunar module in Earth orbit, and Apollo 10 flew a full dress rehearsal in lunar orbit in May 1969.

Apollo 11 launched on 16 July 1969 with Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins. On 20 July the lunar module Eagle, flown by Armstrong and Aldrin, landed in the Sea of Tranquility while Collins orbited in the command module Columbia. Armstrong became the first person to walk on the Moon, followed by Aldrin; they spent about two and a half hours outside, collected 21.5 kilograms of samples and deployed experiments including a seismometer and a laser reflector. The crew splashed down in the Pacific Ocean on 24 July and was recovered by the aircraft carrier USS Hornet. Apollo 12 landed in November 1969 near the Surveyor 3 probe, and Pete Conrad and Alan Bean retrieved parts of it for study.

Apollo 13, launched in April 1970 with Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise, was aborted after an oxygen tank in the service module exploded on the way to the Moon. The crew used the lunar module Aquarius as a lifeboat, and flight director Gene Kranz and the Mission Control team improvised procedures that brought the astronauts home safely. Apollo 14, commanded by Alan Shepard in 1971, reached the Fra Mauro highlands. Apollo 15 introduced the Lunar Roving Vehicle, built by Boeing, which let David Scott and James Irwin explore the Hadley Rille region. Apollo 16 landed in the Descartes Highlands in 1972.

Apollo 17, launched in December 1972, was the final lunar landing. Commander Gene Cernan and geologist Harrison Schmitt, the only professional scientist to walk on the Moon, spent three days in the Taurus-Littrow valley while Ronald Evans orbited above. Cernan was the last person to leave the lunar surface. In total, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon across six landings and brought back 382 kilograms of lunar rock and soil. Apollo hardware later launched the Skylab space station in 1973 and flew the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project with the Soviet Union in 1975, the first international crewed space mission. The program cost about 25.4 billion dollars, employed some 400,000 people at its peak, and its Saturn V remains the most powerful rocket successfully flown until the 2020s.
